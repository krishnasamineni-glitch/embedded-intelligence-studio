clip: anim_01.mp4 || Hi, and welcome! Let me start with a confession. When I write a review paper, the writing is not the hardest part. The hardest part is the bookkeeping. Hundreds of search results, the same paper appearing three times, PDFs with strange file names, and references that never match. That is exactly the part a computer can do for me. So in this video I will walk you through my whole process, from the first search to the final draft, with a small Python tool that does the boring work. My example topic is antenna research, so you will see real antenna papers on the screen.
clip: anim_02.mp4 || Before the details, let me show you the whole journey on one slide. It starts with a plan, your question and your rules. Then you search the databases and bring all the results into one table, where the tool removes the duplicates. After that you screen the papers, and collect the PDFs of the ones that survive. Those PDFs become small Word files for the AI, and the AI writes a draft that cites papers only by number. A checker turns those numbers into real references, and then you revise again and again. Look at the colours: green means the tool does it, amber means I decide. And the decisions always stay with me.
clip: anim_03.mp4 || So, where does it all begin? With the search. And a good search starts on paper, not in the database. For my example, I am looking for stacked patch antennas, and I care about isolation between the elements, which people also call decoupling. So I write each idea as a group of synonyms joined by OR, and I join the two groups with AND. An AI can help you find more synonyms, but always test them yourself. Once the logic is clear, you write it in each database's own language. PubMed marks the fields with the tiab tag, and Scopus uses TITLE-ABS-KEY. Other databases work the same way, so this logic travels with you.
clip: anim_04.mp4 || Now the search is running, and you have results. What next? Export them, and keep each database in its own folder. For PubMed, choose the PubMed format. For Scopus, choose CSV, and make sure the abstracts are included, because we need them later for screening. And while the export is fresh, write down the database, the date, and the exact search string. It takes one minute now, and it will save you later, because the PRISMA diagram and your methods section both need those details.
clip: import_dedupe_synced.mp4 || Now we meet the tool. The first command takes every export you saved and puts all the records into one table. Watch what it prints: for each database, how many files and how many records. That is a safety check, because a broken export shows up immediately and not three days later. In this example I also added a small IEEE export, just to show that other databases work the same way. Then the second command looks for duplicates, and here three hundred and twenty two records shrink to three hundred and one unique papers.
clip: anim_06.mp4 || You may wonder how the tool decides that two records are the same paper. It tries the strongest clue first, the DOI. If that fails, it compares the title and the year, and then it looks for a very close title with the same first author. When it finds a match, it keeps the most complete record. But there is one case where it stops and asks you. Suppose a preprint and the final published paper have different DOIs. The tool does not merge them, because a wrong merge would silently delete a paper. It puts the pair in a list, and you decide.
clip: anim_07.mp4 || After the duplicates, we reach the part that must stay with you, the screening. The tool helps a little: it flags records with no abstract, a wrong year, or none of your keywords. But the decision is yours. You read the title and the abstract, and mark each paper include, exclude, or maybe, with a short reason, because you will need those reasons soon. And every paper that passes gets a permanent number, like P001. Remember this number, because it becomes the only name the AI will ever use for that paper.
clip: anim_08.mp4 || And here is a nice reward for all that logging. Because every step is recorded, the tool can give you the numbers for your PRISMA diagram directly: how many records you found, how many duplicates you removed, how many you screened, and why papers were excluded. The numbers on this slide are only an example. Yours will come from your own files, and you never have to count by hand.
clip: batches_synced.mp4 || So now you know which papers you want. The next job is to get the full texts. The tool checks which papers are open access, and builds simple web pages with ten papers each. Here is one. Every row shows the paper number, the title, and the file name to use when you save the PDF. One click on Open all tabs opens the whole batch, so you are not searching paper by paper. Open access papers come first. For the subscription papers, I log in to my library first, and then open the next batch. And I download every PDF myself, because publishers do not allow bulk downloading by robots.
clip: anim_10.mp4 || Once the PDFs are downloaded, just drop all of them into one inbox folder, with any file names. The tool then matches every file to its paper by the number, the DOI, or the title, renames it, and moves it into the journal or the conference folder. If a file cannot be matched, it is set aside, and you also get a list of the papers still missing, so you know exactly what to download next.
clip: pack_synced.mp4 || With the PDFs in order, we can prepare them for the AI. The pack command converts them into compact Word files, split into small parts, and it keeps the main papers apart from the supplementary ones. As you can see here, it also warns you when it cannot find the references section of a paper. So open one part and spot-check it before you upload anything. And before you upload, check your university's rules and the publishers' rules on AI tools.
clip: anim_12.mp4 || Now the AI has the papers, but it still needs to know what you want. That is the job of the brief file. The tool creates a template, and you fill in the blanks: the type of review, your research questions, the sections you want, the word counts, how the papers should be analysed, and what your own contribution is. Please spend ten minutes here, because a clear brief saves many rounds later, and a vague brief gives you a vague draft.
clip: cite_synced.mp4 || Then the AI writes a draft, and here comes the most important rule. I never let the AI write references, because it can invent papers that sound completely real. Instead, it cites papers only by their numbers, like P012. When the draft is ready, I run the cite command. It turns those numbers into a proper reference list, and it creates a file for your reference manager. And look at this line. I had put a fake tag, P ninety nine, in my draft, and the tool caught it. That is exactly why I trust this step.
clip: anim_14.mp4 || After that, the real work begins, the loop. Read the draft in Word, highlight the weak parts, and add your comments. Save it as the next version, and run the comments command. It collects all your comments and highlights into the next prompt for the AI. Then you read the new draft and repeat. For me, fifteen to twenty rounds is normal. It sounds like a lot, but each round is quick, because the tool does the copying and pasting.
clip: anim_15.mp4 || Before I finish, let me share the rules I follow. The AI is a helper, not an author, so decisions and responsibility stay with you. Verify every fact and every reference, because AI can invent both. The first-draft figures are rarely clear, so I redraw them myself, and I will cover that in another video. And follow your journal's rules on AI use, and say clearly how you used it.
clip: anim_16.mp4 || That is the whole journey, from the first search to the final draft. The tool and the step-by-step guides are linked in the description. If this helped, please subscribe, and tell me in the comments which step you would like me to explain in more detail. See you in the next video.
